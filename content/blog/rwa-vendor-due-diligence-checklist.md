The demo goes well. The proposal looks polished. Your launch date is approaching, and the vendor says it can cover everything. Before you sign, ask a less comfortable question: if the service stopped tomorrow, could your team recover the records, control the assets and keep serving investors?

Due diligence turns that question into a practical buying process. You do not need to ask every provider for the same mountain of documents. You need evidence for the responsibilities you are handing over, starting with the twelve checks below.

## Twelve checks and the evidence to request

| Check | Evidence to request | Hold the decision when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal identity | Registered name, jurisdiction, ownership and signing entity | The proposal and contract name different entities without explanation |
| Permissions | Exact entity, register entry, permitted activity and restrictions | A group licence is offered as proof for a different entity |
| Service boundary | Included services, exclusions and third-party responsibilities | Coordination is described as direct service provision |
| Customer references | Permission to speak to relevant customers | Logos are the only implementation evidence |
| Security assurance | Report date, system boundary, exceptions and remediation | An old or unrelated report is used to imply current coverage |
| Key and admin control | Signer matrix, recovery process and change approvals | No one can name who can move assets or upgrade contracts |
| Contract audits | Report, deployed version and unresolved findings | An audit of different code is presented as coverage |
| Insurance | Insured entity, limits, exclusions and claims process | Marketing language replaces policy evidence |
| Data protection | Locations, processors, retention, access and incident terms | Investor data responsibilities are unspecified |
| Financial continuity | Available accounts, support commitments and contingency plan | No plan exists for provider failure |
| Exit | Register and data exports, migration support and notice | You cannot obtain usable records outside the platform |
| Total cost | Setup, recurring, transaction and pass-through charges | Important operating work is excluded from the quote |

Start with legal identity, activity scope and control of keys. A material unanswered question should remain open even if the overall score looks attractive. This is a procurement checklist, not a promise that a short review establishes regulatory compliance.

## Turn claims into a reproducible evidence log

Use columns for claim, source, exact product or entity, document date, date accessed, reviewer, unresolved question and decision. Keep a source link even when the page is inaccessible. An accessible register homepage is not evidence that the named company has a particular permission.

For security, the [NIST Cybersecurity Framework](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework) can help organize the control discussion. A framework mapping is not certification. For token logic, compare the deployed contracts and configuration with the auditor's actual scope.

## Rehearse one failure and one exit

Ask the vendor to demonstrate a lost signer, failed payment or unavailable data feed. Record how the incident is detected, who authorizes remediation and how the customer is informed. Then ask for a complete export and have a second person reproduce a balance or corporate action without the vendor dashboard.

Do not put real investor personal information in a shared demo environment. Use synthetic records and check who can access the resulting exports.

## Make the decision explicit

Classify each requirement as evidenced, not applicable, open or failed. Assign an owner and deadline to open items. Obtain contractual commitments for dependencies that could prevent launch, and reject a proposal when a critical control cannot be established. Review timing depends on complexity and vendor responsiveness; there is no universal one-week completion promise.
