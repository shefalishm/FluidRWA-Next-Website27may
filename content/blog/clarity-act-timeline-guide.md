## The status in one minute

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act of 2025, usually called the **CLARITY Act**, is a US proposal for the regulation of digital commodities and their intermediaries. The [House passed H.R. 3633 on July 17, 2025](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2025199). It did **not** become law then. On [September 15, 2026, the Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1192/vote_119_2_00234.htm), with 49 in favor, 50 against and one senator not voting. That vote concerned whether to advance consideration, not final passage.

The distinction matters. A platform cannot rely on proposed registration routes, exemptions or definitions as if they were in force. The [House-passed text](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr3633/BILLS-119hr3633eh.pdf) is the best source for what the House approved; Senate committee material records what senators subsequently proposed. Neither is a substitute for current law or advice on a particular product.

| Date | Documented event | What it did not do |

| June 11, 2025 | The [House Financial Services Committee voted 32-19 and the House Agriculture Committee 47-6](https://financialservices.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=409764) to advance it. | Committee votes were not final congressional passage. |

| November 10, 2025 | Senate Agriculture leaders [released a bipartisan discussion draft](https://www.agriculture.senate.gov/newsroom/rep/press/release/boozman-booker-release-bipartisan-market-structure-discussion-draft). | The committee described open issues; the draft was not law. |

| September 15, 2026 | The [Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed, 49-50](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1192/vote_119_2_00234.htm). | It was not a final roll-call vote on passage. |

**As checked October 25, 2026:** no enacted CLARITY Act is established by these records. Any later vote or substituted text would require a new status check.

## What is in the House-passed bill?

The [258-page House-passed text](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr3633/BILLS-119hr3633eh.pdf) is the reference version for this summary. Its table of contents separates the bill into six titles:

| Part of the House-passed text | What a reader will find |
| --- | --- |
| Title I: definitions and implementation | Proposed definitions, rulemaking, expedited intermediary registration and savings provisions. |
| Title II: offers and sales | Proposed treatment of certain investment-contract-related digital commodity transactions, disclosures and blockchain maturity requirements. |
| Title III: SEC intermediaries | Proposed rules touching securities intermediaries, alternative trading systems, recordkeeping and custody-related activities. |
| Title IV: CFTC intermediaries | Proposed regulation of digital commodity exchanges, brokers, dealers and qualified custodians. |
| Title V: innovation | Proposed innovation offices and studies, including on decentralized finance and market infrastructure. |
| Title VI: anti-CBDC provisions | The House-passed version also contains the Anti-CBDC Surveillance State Act. |

This is an outline of the **proposal**, not a statement that any of these provisions now governs a business. The precise definition, exception and effective-date language matters; do not base an implementation decision on a headline or this table.

## Why the Senate versions require separate reading

The House bill is one legislative text. Senate Banking and Senate Agriculture worked on related but distinct material. The [November 2025 Agriculture draft](https://www.agriculture.senate.gov/newsroom/rep/press/release/boozman-booker-release-bipartisan-market-structure-discussion-draft) discussed a CFTC spot-market framework, customer protections and coordination with the SEC, while acknowledging unresolved language. Senate Banking then [released revised text in May 2026](https://www.banking.senate.gov/newsroom/majority/chairman-scott-senators-lummis-tillis-release-market-structure-bill-text-ahead-of-banking-committee-markup) for its own process.

When a commentary says "the CLARITY Act would require X," check **which version and section** it means. A proposal can change in committee, on the floor or between chambers. The September 2026 Senate vote does not itself tell us which provisions would have survived into any future law.

## What does this mean for tokenized real-world assets?

For RWA teams, the first question remains **what legal claim the token represents**. A token may record a fund interest, equity, debt, a beneficial interest or another right. Calling it an RWA or placing it on a blockchain does not answer whether an offer, sale or secondary transaction involves a security. The House text's treatment of digital commodities and its securities-law provisions are reasons to examine the actual rights and transaction, not a shortcut around that examination.

This is FluidRWA's practical reading of the bill's scope, **not a legal classification of any asset**. Before choosing issuance, transfer-agent, custody, trading or distribution vendors, prepare a short product map for counsel:

1. Identify the legal instrument, issuer and rights held by token owners.
2. Map primary issuance, redemption and secondary transfers separately.
3. Name each intermediary and who controls assets and customer records at each step.
4. Record investor location, marketing geography and any transfer restrictions.
5. Note which assumptions depend on a *proposed* CLARITY provision rather than current law.

## What should readers watch next?

Watch for a new Senate floor action, revised committee text, an amendment to H.R. 3633, or a compromise version approved by both chambers. Use the [Senate roll-call record](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_votes/vote1192/vote_119_2_00234.htm) for vote outcomes, the [Congress.gov bill page](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/3633) for the legislative file, and the actual bill text for substantive provisions. Congress.gov's page may lag a fresh procedural vote; cross-check the Senate's own roll call before repeating a status claim.
