# FluidRWA blog voice

Write for someone trying to make a real purchase, launch, or operating decision. A useful article should answer the question in its title before it explains the market around it.

## Before drafting

- Identify the reader and the decision they are making. A fund issuer, developer, compliance lead, and investor need different answers.
- Check the primary sources behind current product claims. Keep the source link near the claim and name what the documentation does not establish.
- Choose one concrete example or workflow to carry the article. Do not invent a customer, result, quote, benchmark, or first-hand test.
- Keep publication, fact-check, and prose-edit dates distinct. A copy edit does not refresh a vendor claim.

## On the page

- Open with the answer or the immediate buyer problem. Skip throat-clearing about a fast-moving industry.
- Use ordinary words when they are precise. Explain specialist terms once, then use them consistently.
- Tell the reader what to ask, request, test, or compare. Name who owns a failed payment, rejected investor, lost key, or disputed record.
- Give tables a purpose. Each row should help someone choose or verify something; avoid generic columns repeated across unrelated markets.
- End with a concrete next step. Do not repeat the introduction as a conclusion.

## Edit out

- Formulaic contrasts such as "not X, but Y" when a direct sentence works.
- Filler openings: "here's the thing," "the real question is," "it's important to note," and "in today's fast-paced world."
- Identical diligence paragraphs under every vendor, repeated RFP lists, and FAQs that merely restate the article.
- Unsupported superlatives, invented rankings, vague promises, and claims that product documentation proves real-world performance.
- Dense three-part lists when one specific example would make the point clearer.

Before publishing, read the first two paragraphs aloud. If they could introduce a different article by replacing the company names, rewrite them.
